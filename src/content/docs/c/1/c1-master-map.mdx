<Eyebrow>The idea in one sentence</Eyebrow>
## A misstated debit means a misstated credit
The slides on directional testing (Ch 7) give the whole map in one line: directional testing derives from **double entry**. A misstated debit means a misstated credit, or an opposite misstatement of another debit. So **testing one side gives evidence about the other**. Strong auditors do not learn receivables, revenue and cash as three topics; they learn one link and three ways to look at it.

<Eyebrow>How to study with the map</Eyebrow>
## Three loops, ten minutes a day
1. **Say it before you click.** Pick an account. Out loud: its twin, its risk direction, three procedures. Then open the panel and mark yourself.
2. **Predict the ripple.** Read a Ripple story, write which balances move and whether profit is over- or understated, then switch to the scenario.
3. **One test, many balances.** Pick a lens and list every balance it covers before you look.

This is the order the exam rewards: **risk → assertion → procedure** (CP4, CP5 Skill A). The models behind each step are in [C2 · Audit operating system](/c/2/c2-operating-system/).

<KeyPrinciple>**Test one side, conclude on both:** choose the test that gives evidence over the account AND its twin, in the right direction.</KeyPrinciple>

<QuickCheck lang="en" q="A GRN dated 29 September (year end 30 September) has no invoice and no accrual. Which balances are misstated?" short="Purchases and payables (and check the goods are in inventory)">
All purchases for which goods have been received before the year end must be included as a liability, an expense (purchases) and closing inventory (Ch 13 §2.5). With no invoice and no accrual, payables and purchases are understated; the GRN cut-off test also checks that the goods are in closing inventory.
</QuickCheck>
